Banking Law and Usage

UNISA · LML4807 · LLB Module

Areas covered in the module

The module examined the legal foundations of banking and the regulation of financial transactions, institutions and customer relationships.

01
Foundations and Sources of Banking Law

The nature and scope of banking law, its constitutional context and its sources, including legislation, English law, common law, contract, negotiable instruments, customary practices, stokvels and Islamic banking principles.

02
The South African Banking System

The establishment and functions of the South African Reserve Bank, its constitutional position, monetary responsibilities, currency and cash reserves, lender-of-last-resort role, and the regulation and registration of banks.

03
Financial Inclusion

Access to and use of financial services, quality of financial products, barriers to inclusion, microfinance, stokvels, affordability, consumer protection and the relationship between inclusion and financial stability.

04
The Bank-Customer Relationship

The legal nature of the relationship between a bank and its customer, including the debtor-creditor relationship, duties of care, confidentiality, contractual obligations and the legal treatment of money deposited with a bank.

05
Deposits and Banking Accounts

The legal principles relating to deposits, including depositum and mutuum, bank liability, savings and current accounts, fixed deposits, repayment arrangements and advances against fixed deposits.

06
The National Payment System

The National Payment System Act, the role of the SARB and PASA, clearing and settlement, clearing house agreements, payment service arrangements, credit cards, debit orders and stop orders.

07
Electronic Banking

Electronic funds transfers, EFT point-of-sale transactions, internet and mobile banking, smart cards, electronic money, payment reversals, countermanding payments and risks associated with unauthorised electronic transactions.

08
Credit Agreements and Consumer Protection

The National Credit Act 34 of 2005, regulated credit agreements, credit providers, debt counsellors, credit bureaux, consumer rights, affordability, reckless credit, over-indebtedness, interest, fees, disclosure, debt collection and enforcement.

09
Money Laundering Control

The Prevention of Organised Crime Act, the Financial Intelligence Centre Act, the Financial Intelligence Centre, accountable institutions, customer identification and verification, record keeping, transaction reporting, suspicious transaction reporting, offences and compliance responsibilities.

10
Financial Sector Regulation

The 2008 global financial crisis, the Twin Peaks regulatory model, the Financial Sector Regulation Act, financial stability, the Prudential Authority, the Financial Sector Conduct Authority and regulatory cooperation.

How the module developed my understanding

Studying Banking Law and Usage helped me connect legal principles with the regulatory responsibilities of financial institutions. It gave me a foundation for understanding how financial services are regulated, how customer interests are protected and why compliance is essential to the integrity of the financial system.

01
Understanding the Legal and Regulatory Framework

I developed an understanding of how banking law is shaped by constitutional principles, legislation, common law and contractual obligations. I learned that financial institutions operate within a framework of legal duties, regulatory requirements and supervisory oversight.

This helped me understand why regulatory compliance requires more than knowing individual rules. It also requires understanding where legal obligations originate, how they apply and how institutions must incorporate them into their operations.

02
The Role of Financial Regulators

I gained an understanding of the role of the South African Reserve Bank in monetary policy and financial stability, as well as its responsibilities within the banking and payment-system environment.

I also studied the development of the Twin Peaks regulatory model and the distinction between prudential supervision and market-conduct regulation. This introduced me to the respective roles of the Prudential Authority and the Financial Sector Conduct Authority.

I learned why effective supervision, regulatory cooperation and institutional accountability are important to a stable and trustworthy financial sector.

03
Banking Operations and Customer Relationships

I developed an understanding of the legal relationship between a bank and its customer, including the contractual and debtor-creditor principles that generally apply to deposits.

I studied the responsibilities banks may owe to customers, including duties relating to care and confidentiality, and considered the legal implications of account opening, deposits, withdrawals, fixed deposits and repayment arrangements.

This helped me appreciate how legal obligations influence everyday banking activities and the way financial institutions manage customer accounts.

04
Payment Systems and Electronic Banking

I learned about the legal and institutional framework supporting South Africa's National Payment System, including the roles of the SARB, payment-system participants and clearing arrangements.

I also studied the legal issues associated with electronic funds transfers, card payments, internet banking, mobile banking and electronic money. This included considering payment reversals, countermanding payment instructions and the risks associated with unauthorised transactions.

These topics developed my awareness of the legal responsibilities and operational risks associated with modern financial services.

05
Consumer Credit Regulation and Responsible Lending

Studying the National Credit Act 34 of 2005 helped me understand the regulatory framework governing credit agreements and the protection of consumers in the credit market.

I developed knowledge of affordability assessments, reckless credit, over-indebtedness, disclosure requirements, interest and charges, consumer rights, debt counselling and the enforcement of credit agreements.

I also considered the importance of protecting personal information and credit records within the credit environment. These topics highlighted the importance of fair treatment, responsible lending and compliance with consumer protection requirements.

06
Financial Intelligence Centre Act (FICA)

I developed an understanding of the Financial Intelligence Centre Act 38 of 2001 (FICA), South Africa's primary legislative framework for combating money laundering and terrorist financing within accountable institutions.

I learned that FICA places specific compliance responsibilities on accountable institutions to help prevent their products and services from being misused for unlawful activities. These responsibilities extend beyond identifying customers and include ongoing monitoring, record keeping, regulatory reporting, risk management and internal compliance controls.

My understanding includes the importance of:

  • Identifying and verifying clients and relevant persons.
  • Understanding the nature and intended purpose of business relationships.
  • Establishing beneficial ownership and understanding ownership structures.
  • Conducting ongoing customer due diligence and transaction monitoring.
  • Maintaining customer and transaction records.
  • Identifying and reporting suspicious or unusual transactions.
  • Applying targeted financial sanctions requirements.
  • Implementing a Risk Management and Compliance Programme (RMCP).

I also developed an appreciation of how FICA works alongside other legislation, particularly the Prevention of Organised Crime Act 121 of 1998 (POCA), in addressing the proceeds of unlawful activities and financial crime.

This knowledge helped me understand that FICA compliance is an ongoing institutional responsibility rather than a once-off customer identification exercise.

07
The Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC)

I developed an understanding of the role of the Financial Intelligence Centre as South Africa's financial intelligence unit. The FIC receives, analyses and disseminates financial intelligence to assist in identifying the proceeds of crime and combating money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing.

I learned the distinction between the FIC and accountable institutions. Financial institutions and other accountable institutions are responsible for identifying risks, applying customer due diligence, monitoring transactions and submitting required reports. The FIC uses financial intelligence to support relevant authorities and the broader anti-financial-crime framework.

My understanding of the FIC's responsibilities includes:

  • Receiving regulatory reports from accountable institutions.
  • Analysing financial information and identifying potential patterns of suspicious activity.
  • Disseminating financial intelligence to relevant law enforcement, intelligence and supervisory authorities.
  • Registering accountable institutions and administering the FIC reporting system.
  • Supporting compliance with FICA obligations through guidance and regulatory communication.
  • Supervising and enforcing compliance within its statutory mandate.
  • Supporting the implementation of targeted financial sanctions.

I also learned why accurate, complete and timely reporting is important. Financial intelligence depends on institutions recognising potentially suspicious activity and fulfilling their reporting responsibilities.

08
Know Your Customer (KYC)

I developed an understanding of Know Your Customer (KYC) as a fundamental process used by financial institutions to establish who their customers are, understand their circumstances and assess the risks associated with providing financial products and services.

KYC is important because financial institutions must understand who they are dealing with before entering into a business relationship and must maintain sufficient knowledge of their customers throughout that relationship.

My understanding of KYC includes the importance of:

  • Establishing the identity of individual and business customers.
  • Verifying customer information using appropriate and reliable information sources.
  • Understanding the customer's occupation, business activities and expected financial behaviour.
  • Understanding why a customer wants to establish a particular business relationship or use a financial product.
  • Identifying persons acting on behalf of customers and verifying their authority.
  • Identifying beneficial owners behind legal entities and ownership structures.
  • Screening customers for relevant politically exposed or prominent influential person status and sanctions exposure.
  • Keeping customer information accurate and updated.

I learned that KYC is not simply about collecting identity documents. It involves developing a meaningful understanding of the customer so that the institution can assess whether the customer's activities are consistent with their known profile.

This is particularly important in preventing identity fraud, misuse of financial products, concealment of beneficial ownership and the use of financial institutions for illicit purposes.

09
Customer Due Diligence (CDD)

I developed an understanding of Customer Due Diligence as a central FICA compliance requirement. CDD involves obtaining and assessing information about a customer so that an accountable institution understands the customer, the intended business relationship and the risks associated with that relationship.

I learned that CDD extends beyond verifying a customer's identity. Under FICA, institutions must also obtain information that enables them to understand the nature and purpose of a business relationship and the expected source of funds.

My understanding of the main CDD elements includes:

  • Customer identification: Establishing the identity of the customer and relevant persons involved in the relationship.
  • Identity verification: Confirming customer identity using appropriate information and verification measures.
  • Purpose and nature of the relationship: Understanding why the customer is opening an account or entering into a particular financial relationship.
  • Source of funds: Understanding where the money used in the business relationship or transactions originates.
  • Beneficial ownership: Identifying the natural persons who ultimately own or control a legal entity or arrangement.
  • Risk assessment: Evaluating the customer's circumstances, activities, products and other relevant factors to determine the level of money laundering and terrorist financing risk.
  • Ongoing due diligence: Reviewing customer information and monitoring transactions to ensure that activity remains consistent with the institution's understanding of the customer.

I also learned about the importance of keeping CDD records and reviewing information when circumstances change, previously obtained information becomes doubtful or unusual activity is identified.

This helped me understand how CDD supports customer risk management and enables institutions to identify transactions that may require further investigation.

10
Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD)

I developed an understanding of Enhanced Due Diligence as additional scrutiny and verification measures applied where a customer or business relationship presents a higher level of risk. It forms part of the risk-based approach to anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing compliance.

I learned that customers should not all be assessed in exactly the same way. The extent of due diligence should be proportionate to the risks identified by the institution. Higher-risk relationships may therefore require additional information, further verification and closer monitoring.

Situations that may require enhanced scrutiny include:

  • Customers presenting higher money laundering or terrorist financing risks.
  • Politically exposed persons (PEPs), prominent influential persons (PIPs), and relevant family members or known close associates, as applicable under FICA.
  • Complex ownership structures or difficulty identifying beneficial owners.
  • Unusual transaction patterns or activity inconsistent with the customer's known profile.
  • Higher-risk products, services, business relationships or geographic exposure.
  • Transactions involving unusual complexity, significant value or no apparent lawful or economic purpose.

Enhanced measures may include obtaining additional customer information, establishing source of funds and, where appropriate, source of wealth, conducting further verification, obtaining appropriate senior management approval and applying more frequent or detailed transaction monitoring.

I also learned the importance of distinguishing between source of funds and source of wealth. Source of funds concerns where the money for a particular transaction or relationship originates, while source of wealth concerns how the customer's overall wealth was acquired.

This strengthened my understanding of how risk-based compliance controls help financial institutions identify, assess and manage higher-risk relationships rather than relying solely on standard customer onboarding checks.

11
Transaction Monitoring and Suspicious Transaction Reporting

I developed an understanding of the importance of monitoring customer transactions throughout a business relationship. Transaction monitoring assists institutions in identifying activity that may be inconsistent with the customer's known profile or that may indicate suspicious or unusual behaviour.

I learned why institutions must consider transaction patterns, frequency, value, counterparties and the purpose of activity when reviewing potential concerns.

My understanding includes the importance of identifying unusual transactions, reviewing alerts, documenting findings and escalating concerns through the appropriate internal processes.

I also developed awareness of reporting obligations under FICA, including suspicious and unusual transaction reports, cash threshold reports and other prescribed regulatory reports where applicable.

This highlighted the importance of accurate documentation, confidentiality and timely escalation in financial crime prevention.

12
POCA and the Prevention of Financial Crime

I studied the Prevention of Organised Crime Act 121 of 1998 and its role in addressing organised crime, money laundering and the proceeds of unlawful activities.

I developed an understanding of money-laundering-related offences and the legal framework for confiscating and forfeiting proceeds of crime.

This helped me understand the relationship between preventative compliance obligations under FICA and the broader legal measures used to investigate and address financial crime.

13
Financial Inclusion and Consumer Protection

I explored the importance of access to financial services and the barriers that may prevent individuals and communities from using appropriate financial products.

I learned that financial inclusion must be considered alongside consumer protection, affordability, financial stability and the integrity of the financial system. The module also examined the role of stokvels, microfinance and other forms of community-based financial activity.

This helped me understand the importance of balancing regulatory safeguards with access to financial services.

14
Applying Legal Knowledge to Compliance

Across the module, I strengthened my ability to identify legal obligations, interpret legislation and consider how regulatory requirements affect the conduct of financial institutions.

I developed a broader understanding of how compliance supports institutional accountability, responsible financial services, customer protection and confidence in the financial system.

This academic foundation supports my continued development towards a career in regulatory compliance, FICA, AML, KYC and financial crime prevention within the financial services sector.

Regulatory Framework

Understanding the Twin Peaks Model

South Africa's Twin Peaks model separates financial-sector regulation into two main regulatory functions.

Peak One

FSCA

Focuses on how financial institutions treat customers, conduct business and maintain fair and transparent market practices.

Peak Two

Prudential Authority

Focuses on the financial soundness, stability and resilience of regulated financial institutions.

Both regulators operate within the broader financial stability framework, with the South African Reserve Bank playing a central role.
